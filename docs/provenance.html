Where Every Number Comes From

The Sphere shows numbers — fish counted at a dam, blossoms recorded in Kyoto, the sun worked out for your own place. This page is the receipt shelf: for each set of numbers, what it is, who publishes it, what years it covers, when we read it, and what is honestly missing. If a number on the Sphere cannot be traced to a line on this page, it does not belong on the Sphere.

Plain words first. The publishers' own names for their data stand in each card, one step down, for anyone who wants to check at the source.

How to read a card

  • Counted — somebody stood there and counted, year after year. The number is a record.
  • Computed — worked out by mathematics on your own device, from the real motions of the sun, moon, and planets. Nothing is fetched and nothing can go stale.
  • Kept — Aaron's own record: his study years and kept days. It is labelled as his wherever it stands, and never mixed with the counted or computed sets.
  • The gaps are part of the record. Where a source has missing years, this page says so, and the Sphere draws the gap as a gap.

How the Sphere itself works — the calendar, the sky, and the labels it uses — is on the Methods page.

Salmon at Bonneville — counted

Every autumn and spring, fish swim up the Columbia River past Bonneville Dam, and they are counted, one by one, day by day. The Sphere's salmon layer draws those counts: pick a day, and the same day in other years answers with the count that was recorded on it.

What it isDaily counts of adult salmon and steelhead passing Bonneville Dam on the Columbia River, for four runs: Chinook, steelhead, sockeye, and coho. Young fish (jacks) are not included, and the wild-steelhead column is a part of the steelhead count, not an extra count.
Who counts and publishesThe counts are made by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers at the dam and published by Columbia River DART (Columbia Basin Research, University of Washington) as its Adult Passage Daily Counts.
Years covered1939–2026. Counted days held: Chinook 23,303 · steelhead 25,769 · sockeye 9,232 · coho 5,815 (coho from 1980 — see the gaps).
When we read itNovember 23, 2026 — one file per calendar year, taken from DART's own tables. Re-reading the source is a deliberate act; the copy on the Sphere carries its read date.
Checked againstA day any reader can look up: September 1, 2023 reads Chinook 41,510 and steelhead 3,092 in DART's published table, and the same in the Sphere's copy.

The honest gaps. DART labels Bonneville “[1938]” — the dam's first counting year — but serves no daily counts for 1938, so the record here begins in 1939 and says so. Coho were not counted separately before 1980: the coho column is empty for every day of 1939–1979, so the Sphere holds coho from 1980 and draws the earlier years as gaps. A few published counts are negative (a correction the counters themselves printed); they are kept as published, not tidied away.

Kyoto cherry blossoms — counted, across twelve centuries

For over a thousand years, people in Kyoto wrote down the day the cherry trees reached full bloom — in court diaries, temple chronicles, and modern records. It is the longest single thread the Sphere can hold: one city, one tree, one day a year.

What it isThe date of full flowering of the cherry blossoms in Kyoto, Japan, one date per year.
Who keeps itThe record was gathered by Yasuyuki Aono (Osaka Prefecture University) from historical documents and modern observation. The Sphere's copy reads his own workbook for the years to 2015, and Our World in Data's continuation of the same record (published under CC BY 4.0) for 2016–2026.
Years covered812–2026 — 838 years carry a recorded date. The earliest full bloom in the record is March 25, 2023; the latest, May 4, 1323.
When we read itNovember 23, 2026. The university's own page did not answer, so Aono's workbook was read from the Internet Archive's saved copy of his page; his name stands in the file's own record of who made it.

The honest gaps. Of the 1,215 years from 812 to 2026, 377 have no surviving record — no document, no date. On the Sphere the thread breaks where the record breaks; a missing year is drawn as a missing year. One correction, made openly: for 1923, Aono's workbook gives the year's 97th day (April 7) while Our World in Data's current table gives the 98th. Two independent copies of the record agree with Aono, so the Sphere carries 97 — the compiler's own hand outranks a copyist's slip — and the disagreement is written down here rather than smoothed over.

The sky — computed on your device

The sunrises, moon phases, and stars on the Sphere are not downloaded. They are worked out, on the device in your hand, from the real motions of the sun, moon, planets, and stars — so the same sky stands on the website and inside the app, which by design has no internet connection at all.

What it isSunrise, sunset, solar noon, and the length of daylight; the moon's phase and how much of it is lit; when the moon rises and sets; the dates the seasons turn, for any year; where the planets stand; and 510 bright stars placed over your place.
Computed fromStandard published astronomy, running entirely on the device: the sun's times by the hour-angle method used in the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's solar calculations; the planets from Paul Schlyter's published low-precision elements (their accuracy class is named in the code, not hidden); the stars from the Astronomical Almanac Bright Star List (epoch 2016.5, drawn from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue), keeping every star of magnitude 4.00 or brighter.
Checked againstPublished tables read November 23, 2026: Seattle sun and moon tables (sunrise and sunset within 3 minutes; moonrise and moonset within 10; the moon's lit fraction within 1.5 points), the U.S. Naval Observatory's season table (within 20 minutes), and the equinox table below (within 30 minutes). Three stars whose minus signs were dropped in copying the star list — Sirius, Canopus, and Arcturus — were restored and the correction documented.
The season turnsFor 2026 and 2027 the face has long printed the U.S. Naval Observatory's published instants (read November 20, 2026). The sky engine now finds the turns itself for any year, by watching the sun's longitude cross the turn — and where the Observatory's table stands, the table keeps precedence.
The equinox tableUnder the calendar's study years stands a table of March equinox instants for 2014–2027, copied into the site from a published table (janmr.com) and checked against a second, independent table: the two agree to within about 2 seconds, inside the table's stated 3-second tolerance. Stated plainly: the chain back to the original computation is only partly documented in the site's files, and the file itself says so. It is used as a check, not as an authority.

What the sky does not do. No place kept, no sun times — the Sphere will not guess where you are. Below the horizon, nothing is drawn standing up. And the weather strip (temperature, air, and kin) is a different thing from the computed sky: it is fetched from the Open-Meteo services when a place is kept and the network answers, and when it cannot arrive, the face prints one honest sentence instead of a number.

The farm record — 39 counties of Western Washington

The garden's planting windows — when a crop can go in the ground, county by county — were built from published climate records and extension teaching, not from habit. Every window in the underlying data carries the source it came from.

What it isPlanting windows and frost dates for all 39 Washington counties, entered November 20, 2026. Spring windows are counted in weeks from each county's average last spring frost, the way almanac and extension calendars count them.
Who publishes the ground truthEach county is anchored on a named station in the NOAA U.S. Climate Normals 1991–2020 (the thirty-year climate record published by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) — its published frost dates and frost-free season length. Planting teaching comes from Washington State University Extension (the Snohomish, Skagit, and King County charts and tip sheets, including the fall and winter garden chart and the tomato rule: transplant around mid-May, once the soil is at 50°F or warmer), the Farmer's Almanac seed-starting and frost tables (which count a 30% chance of frost after the date, where NOAA's median counts the middle year — where the two disagree, both answers are kept and the disagreement written into the crop's notes), and the 2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map.
The one computed countyOne county has no NOAA normals station of its own. Its dates are computed from named neighbouring anchors by a stated rule, and its entry says it is computed and names its anchors. Where a county's safe planting line was not itself published, it follows the garden's stated safe rule — ten days after the median last frost — and is labelled a rule, not a measurement.
The teaching shelfBeside the windows stands a teaching set of 42 sourced guides for the garden. A crop or window with no sourced value is left out of the record entirely — never guessed.

The calendar's fixtures — and the study years

The calendar itself is arithmetic, and arithmetic can be checked. The calendar is pinned by fixtures — agreed dates that every copy of the calendar, on the site and in the app, must reproduce exactly, tested by a shared set of test dates both must pass:

  • April 17, 2026 = Moon 1, Day 1, Pattern Day 1.
  • July 17, 2026 = Moon 4, Day 8, Pattern Day 92.
  • September 15, 2026 = Moon 6, Day 12, Pattern Day 152.
  • November 23, 2026 = Moon 8, Day 25, Pattern Day 221.

Beside the kept record stands the alignment ledger: Aaron's own study dataset covering the civil years 2014–2027. Those are study years — years examined, not years any keeper lived inside this calendar — and they stand on the Sphere labelled as study years, carrying only what is truly documented there.

A fuller census of long records examined for the Sphere — sunspots back to 1749, river flow, ice dates six centuries long, and more, including the ones that failed the test and why — is kept with the Sphere's build records. This page lists what the Sphere actually shows.
