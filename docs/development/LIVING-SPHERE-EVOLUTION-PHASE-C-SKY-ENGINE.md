## 1. What computes now that didn't

A new module, `docs/assets/js/astronomy/sky-engine.js` (`CodexSkyEngine`), pure functions of a moment and a place, no network, nothing fetched at runtime:

| Quantity | Method | Stated class (measured, §3) |
|---|---|---|
| Sun position | Meeus low-precision solar coordinates (Astronomical Algorithms ch. 25) | ~0.01° |
| Sunrise, sunset, solar noon, daylight | Hour-angle method on the computed declination, iterated at the event instants; polar day/night named, never faked | ±3 min in tests (class: ±1–2 min temperate) |
| Twilight crossings | The computed Sun's altitude walked and bisected (the dome's nightfall is the −12° crossing) | same solar class |
| Season turns, any year | Root-found crossings of the Sun's apparent ecliptic longitude through 0°/90°/180°/270° | within 20 min of USNO 2026; within 30 min of the JPL table 2014–2027 |
| Moon position | Schlyter's lunar elements + the principal perturbation terms, topocentrically corrected for alt/az | within ~1° |
| Moon phase (name, illumination) | Elongation of the computed Moon from the computed Sun | illumination within ~1%; phase instants within ~±3 h |
| Moonrise / moonset | The computed Moon's altitude walked across the local day | ±10 min |
| Planets (Mercury–Neptune) | Schlyter's low-precision orbital elements — the same published source family the Sphere's existing planetary module labels "low-precision-orbital-elements" | arcminute-class for the bright planets in this era; up to ~1° for Mercury; magnitudes approximate |
| Bright stars | A real catalog (§2), precessed from its epoch to the moment, placed by local sidereal time | arcminute-class for this decade; proper motion ignored (stated in the data file) |

Every result carries its source class in code (`source: "computed"` / `"catalog"`), so a surface can label what it shows. The engine never defaults a place: no place, no sky — the caller's rule and the module's alike.

Two traps worth recording, both caught by the published pins before anything shipped:

- **Schlyter's day count starts at 2000 Jan 0.0 (JD 2451543.5), not J2000.0.** The repo's existing planetary module already counts this way; the engine's first draft didn't, which moved the Moon a day and a half of travel. The fix is commented in the engine where the constant lives.
- **Published Moon alt/az and rise/set values are topocentric.** The Moon is close enough that parallax moves it by up to a degree; the engine now shifts the geocentric vector by the observer's own before converting. Moonset at Seattle improved from 10 minutes off to 3.

## 2. The star catalog

`docs/assets/js/astronomy/bright-stars.js` — **510 stars**, generated by `scripts/build-bright-stars.py` from a real source, credited in the file itself:

- **Source:** the Astronomical Almanac's Bright Star List for **epoch 2016.5**, derived from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, 5th revised edition (Hoffleit & Warren 1991; CDS V/50), as distributed in the `brettonw/YaleBrightStarCatalog` repository (`Almanac-BrightStars-2016.txt`, read 2026-11-23).
- **Subset rule:** every star whose listed V magnitude is a single value ≤ 4.00. Stars listed with a magnitude *range* (Mira and five others) are excluded — a range is not a magnitude. One source line (HR 2180) is garbled in the distribution (declination seconds "5 3"); its magnitude (5.50) is above the limit, so nothing in the subset is lost.
- **Sign restoration, documented in the generator and the data file:** the distributed text drops the minus sign on the list's only three negative magnitudes. The published values are restored — Sirius (HR 2491) −1.46, Canopus (HR 2326) −0.72, Arcturus (HR 5340) −0.04 — and the generator hard-fails if the source values ever differ from the printed positives it expects, so the restoration can never silently misapply.
- **Epoch honesty:** coordinates are epoch 2016.5 (the Almanac list already carries precession and proper motion to that epoch). The engine precesses from 2016.5 to the moment of use and ignores proper motion thereafter — below this layer's stated class for decades, and stated in the data file's header.

## 4. What stands on the face

The Sky layer, riding the existing Sky ring (the face's own charter: lunar, solar, planets, markers, passage). The dome is **absent when the Sky ring is off**, exactly as the season line is; it is a focusable layer, not a new always-on surface.

**The caption's sky line** (`sphere-armillary.js`, unprotected) gains computed words through `buildSkyText`, so the caption and the selected day's packed readout — one builder, both tellings — now say, for any date: the season and the next turn **with its true date for every year** (the USNO table keeps precedence in 2026–2027 as the sourced record; beyond it the turns are computed, and the anchor-date fallback is retired wherever the engine is awake); the Moon's phase name and illumination; and, when a place is kept, that day's sunrise and sunset for the place, in the keeper's own clock, labelled *"computed on this device."* No place kept: no sun times are printed. The fetched environment strip is untouched — it stays weather, under its own honest line.

**The sky dome** (`docs/assets/js/sphere-sky-layer.js` + `sphere-sky-dome.css`, loaded by the companion layer in the farm pattern) stands in the caption deck under the sky line: a horizon chart of the selected day over the keeper's kept place — centre overhead, rim the horizon, drawn as you see it looking up (north at top, east to the left, labelled). It draws:

- the day's **sun arc** and **moon arc** from computed altitudes across the day, clipped at the horizon — what is below the horizon is not drawn standing up;
- the **Sun and Moon standing** at the chosen standing — Day: the Sun at its highest; Night: nightfall (the computed −12° crossing; if the Sun never gets there, the middle of the night, and the line says so) — the Moon drawn as a disc with its computed phase;
- at the Night standing, the **bright stars** (the catalog's 510, those above the horizon at that moment, sized by magnitude, the brightest labelled) and the **planets** standing above the horizon, named;
- three plain lines under the chart: the standing and its moment; sunrise · solar noon · sunset · daylight; the Moon's phase and its rising and setting — each labelled computed on this device.

With no place kept, the dome shows one honest sentence instead of a chart. Rendering is coalesced to animation frames (the armillary's own pattern), recomputed on day/place/standing change and on a one-minute tick.

**Performance cost:** data — bright-stars.js 33.7 KB, sky-engine.js ~29 KB, sphere-sky-layer.js ~20 KB, CSS 1.7 KB, all companion-loaded (they do not raise the Sphere page's HTML-referenced weight). Draw — one SVG, no canvas, no per-frame work: worst case (night standing) ~220–300 star points (220 stood over Seattle in the November test moment) + ~7 planet markers + 2 arc polylines ≈ 320 nodes, rebuilt only on change. One full day-sky computation measures ~0.1 seconds on this machine (the Moon's day-walk dominates); it runs at most once per animation frame while scrubbing, and the dome memoizes the last day computed for the kept place.

## 5. Held back, with reasons

- **The Observatory readouts** still read the protected live-data snapshot. Re-sourcing them to the engine means editing `living-time-observatory-workspace.js` and `living-time-sphere-live-data.js` — both R1.7 protected. **Held proposal:** when Aaron gives the word, live-data takes sunrise/sunset/moonrise/moonset from `CodexSkyEngine` for the kept place (falling back to the fetched values never — the engine needs no network), and the Observatory labels them computed-on-device. The engine was built to make that a small, safe change.
- **The 3D renderer's daylight arc** (its environment terminator) starts from the *fetched* sunrise inside protected renderer code reading the protected live-data snapshot. Same held proposal as above; the engine removes the reason the fetch was ever needed.
- **A star shell inside the 3D scene** (via the renderer's extension host): the scene's axes are Pattern geometry — it has no horizon frame, no north. Wrapping the keeper's horizon sky around the instrument would assert a geometric relationship that does not exist. The dome keeps horizon truth in horizon space. If Aaron wants stars *in* the scene, that is a design decision about what the scene's frame means — his call, not a drive-by. **Held proposal.**

## 7. App parity (report only)

The engine and catalog are pure JavaScript with no DOM and no network — the same files load in a WebView unchanged, which is the whole point of computing on-device: Spark. Living Day carries no INTERNET permission by Aaron's design, so the fetched sky could never stand in the app, and the computed sky can. The port is a later step on Aaron's word: drop `sky-engine.js` + `bright-stars.js` into the app bundle, feed them the app's kept place, and the same dome (or the app's own face furniture) stands there with the same numbers, pinned by the same tests.
