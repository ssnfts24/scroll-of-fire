How the Sphere Knows

The Sphere makes claims: this is the day, this is the moon's phase, this fish passed on this day in 1962. This page says, in plain words, how each kind of claim is produced — so a visitor does not have to take the instrument's word for anything. Where every number comes from is on the Provenance page; this page is about the workings.

One clock, and every room reads it

The whole system keeps time through a single chain, and no page is allowed to do its own date sums:

  • The cursor holds where in time you are standing — the day you have chosen to look at.
  • The Pattern Calendar is the one source of calendar truth: it turns that standing-place into a Moon, a day of the Moon, and a Pattern Day.
  • The one record (the canonical living state) holds what has been kept — your days, your entries — in exactly one place.
  • The carrying layer (the projection bus) takes that one state and hands it to every surface, so the Sphere, the calendar pages, the Today view, and the command surfaces are all showing the same day because they are reading the same record.

In the code's own names, the chain is SOFTemporalCursor → PatternCalendar → Canonical Living State → LivingProjectionBus → Sphere / Calendar / Today / Command. The plain version above is the same chain. When the chain was once broken — a day reachable through three doors holding different contents — that was treated as a defect to repair, not a style. One day, one record, every door.

How a date becomes a Pattern Day

The Pattern Calendar counts a year of thirteen moons, twenty-eight days each: 13 × 28 = 364 days. Those are the Pattern Days, numbered 1 to 364. One more day stands outside the count — the Day Out of Time, which belongs to no moon and no week — and in the fuller reckoning a second outside day, the Deep Time Day, stands with it. A leap arrangement keeps the count true against the civil year.

To turn a civil date into its Pattern Day, the calendar counts from a fixed anchor — April 17, 2026 is Moon 1, Day 1, Pattern Day 1 — and the count is pinned by fixtures: agreed dates (July 17, 2026 = Pattern Day 92; September 15, 2026 = Pattern Day 152) that every implementation, site and app alike, must reproduce, tested against a shared set of dates both must pass. If a copy of the calendar ever disagrees with a fixture, the copy is wrong, and the fixture is how we know.

How the sky is computed

The sky is worked out on your own device, at the moment you look — nothing is fetched, so nothing can be out of date, and the same sky stands in the app, which has no internet by design. Sunrise and sunset come from the sun's hour angle for your kept place; the moon's phase and lit fraction from its position against the sun; the seasons' turns are found by watching the sun's longitude cross the turn, for any year you stand in. The planets use published low-precision elements — good enough to place them honestly, and labelled with their class. The stars are a fixed catalogue of 510 bright stars, placed by the turning of the Earth (sidereal time) over your place.

What "approximate" means here. Some things on the Sphere are exact counts and some are computations, and the difference is printed, not hidden. The older moon model still standing in parts of the site labels itself, in its own code, an approximate synodic phase: it counts the moon's age from a known new moon (January 6, 2000) by the average length of a moon's cycle, 29.530588853 days. That is honest arithmetic and it can be a little off the sky's exact moment — which is why, wherever it speaks, it says approximately. The newer sky engine computes the phase from the moon's actual position and is checked against published tables to within minutes. A label that says approximate is not a weakness in the instrument; it is the instrument telling you which kind of knowing you are looking at.

The day-line: the same day, in other years

Every year of the Pattern Calendar shares the same day positions, so one Pattern Day is the same point in every year. Pick a day, and a line runs from it to the same day last year, and five, ten, twenty, thirty years back — and one year forward. What the line may honestly show depends on the kind of thing:

  • Computed things — the sky, the feasts of the computed calendars — truly run forward, because the mathematics holds next year as surely as last.
  • Recorded things — weather, salmon, your own kept days — stop at today. The forward bead stands open and labelled: no record; the year is not kept yet. Next year's weather drawn on the line would be made up, and this instrument does not make things up.
  • Gaps stay gaps. A year with no record shows an open bead, named as no record — never a nearby value stretched onto the day.

On the pattern graph, threads join a kind of thing across the years: a straight thread is a true rhythm, a leaning thread is drift, a broken thread is a year it did not happen. Threads are labelled honestly — same kind, not the same thing — and a thread claims a real link only where a record's own rhythm links them.

The labels — how this site grades what it says

Everything the Codex states carries a grade, and the grade is part of the statement. These are the labels, explained once, here, as the site's single reference:

Verified recordA document, dataset, or artefact that can be opened and checked — a published table, a signed order, a counted record. The strongest grade: do not take our word; take the record's.
Firsthand observationSeen or measured directly by the one reporting it — a day witnessed, a value read off the instrument. Strong, and bounded by the observer.
Reported statementWhat someone said, carried as what they said — attributed, not endorsed. A report being repeated is not a report being confirmed.
AllegationA claim made against a party or about an event, not yet established. It stands labelled as an allegation until a record settles it, one way or the other.
InferenceA conclusion drawn from the above — reasoning, shown as reasoning. An inference may be sound and still be wrong; it never borrows the grade of what it was drawn from.
Unresolved claimA claim the record does not yet settle. It stays open, in the open, until evidence closes it.

The story has its own, separate labelling, and the two never trade clothes: an episode is labelled a story on its face, the tale and the account are kept in different books, and nothing in the story is ever cited as evidence. Inside the work, claims also move through four levels — Observation, Interpretation, Hypothesis, Declaration — and the discipline is that a level is never quietly upgraded: a pattern noticed is not a cause proven, and a defined equation is not a validated law.

An open question, printed open

The Codex's core line stands in the record in two forms, and they are not reconciled:

  • As its author states it: Reality = Resonance × Intention × Light × Consciousness × Source.
  • As the published site also prints it, in six dimensions: (Energy × Information × Intention × Memory × Flow)^Consciousness.

Which form stands is the author's to settle, and until he does, this page prints both, side by side, as an open question — the way the Codex prints all of its open questions: in the open.
