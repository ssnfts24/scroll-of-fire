אָדָם — adam — Full Hebrew Treatment (Finished, Nov 23, 2026)

Labels follow Aaron's rule: observation / interpretation / hypothesis / declaration are kept separate. This study belongs to a cluster — read it with אֲדָמָה (adamah, the ground) and אֱנוֹשׁ (enosh, the mortal). The three words answer each other, and Genesis plays them against each other on purpose.

1. The word itself

  • Consonantal text: אדם (aleph–dalet–mem)
  • Pointed text: אָדָם
  • Transliteration: ʾāḏām — "ah-DAHM"
  • Strong's / lexicon: H120; TWOT 25a
  • Part of speech: masculine noun
  • Frequency: BDB's entry is headed 560 — the word occurs roughly 560 times. Counts in the 550s appear in other concordances; the method (occurrences vs. verses, inclusion of the proper name) moves the number. Quote it as "about 560" and name the method.
  • Forms: almost always singular in form. There is no true plural of אָדָם in Biblical Hebrew; where English wants "men," Hebrew uses בְּנֵי (הָ)אָדָם, "sons of (the) adam" — literally "children of the human" (Gen 11:5; Ps 8 passim in the singular). The construct form is the same as the absolute except in Prov 6:12. With the article, הָאָדָם, it is "the human / the man" — the generic figure of Genesis 2–3. Without the article, in later texts, it can be the proper name Adam.

2. Root — three candidates, none proven

This word's etymology is genuinely unsettled, and the study must not pretend otherwise.

  1. From אָדַם (ʾāḏam, H119), "to be red / ruddy." Strong's derives אָדָם from this verb: "ruddy, i.e. a human being." The family is real — אָדֹם (ʾāḏōm, "red," H122), אֹדֶם (ʾōḏem, a red gem, sardius/carnelian, H124), דָּם (dām, "blood," H1818) all live in the same sound-neighbourhood. But "related sounds" is not a derivation. The NAS concordance pointedly gives the origin as "an unused word," i.e. it declines Strong's derivation.
  2. From אֲדָמָה (adamah), the ground. The biblical text's own wordplay (Gen 2:7) ties the human to the ground he was formed from. Whether that is historical etymology or the narrator's theological wordplay is debated; as the text's own claim about what a human is, it stands regardless of the etymology question. See the adamah study.
  3. A loan or shared old Semitic word. BDB lists Phoenician אדם and Sabaean (South Arabian) ʾdm with the same meaning, so the word is old and regional, not a Hebrew coinage. BDB also reports an Assyrian admu, "young (of a bird)," proposed by one scholar and disputed by another (Nöldeke) — printed here as what it is: a proposal that did not win agreement.
  • Observation: the lexica disagree, on the record.
  • Do not upgrade: "adam means red" and "adam means earthling" are both preachable lines; neither is a settled lexicon finding. What is settled is the usage — §3.

3. Semantic range (BDB)

  1. A human being — an individual person, male or female (Gen 2:22–25 uses it across the man/woman distinction; the word itself is not the word for "male" — that is אִישׁ, ʾîsh, or זָכָר, zākhār).
  2. Mankind, humankind collectively — Gen 1:26–27; 5:1–2; 9:5–6. Gen 5:2 is explicit: "male and female he created them... and called their name adam." The species-name covers both sexes in the text's own words.
  3. The proper name Adam — the first man as an individual (Gen 4:25; 5:3–5; 1 Chron 1:1). Scholars debate exactly where the generic becomes the name; Genesis 2–3 mostly writes הָאָדָם, "the human," with the article — a figure, not yet a name-tag.
  4. A place name — a city in the Jordan valley (Josh 3:16, H121). Included so the concordance count is honest.

4. Biblical examples, by sense — Genesis first, where the weight is

The species, made in the image — Gen 1:26–27. "Let us make adam in our image... male and female he created them." Here adam is the kind, not an individual, and the image of God is predicated of the kind, both sexes, in one breath.

The groundling, formed and breathed into — Gen 2:7. God forms הָאָדָם from the dust of הָאֲדָמָה and breathes into his nostrils the breath of life (see the nephesh study: the man does not receive a nephesh here — he becomes one). The adam/adamah pairing is the Bible's foundational wordplay on what a human is: ground, animated.

The one who names — Gen 2:19–20. The adam names the animals (see the shem study: naming as knowing and ordering). "But for adam no helper corresponding to him was found" — the word still generic, the loneliness specific.

Return to the ground — Gen 3:19. "Until you return to the adamah, for from it you were taken; for dust (עָפָר, ʿāpār) you are, and to dust you shall return." The wordplay completes its arc: adam from adamah, adam to adamah. Mortality is built into the name-play from the start — which is where אֱנוֹשׁ (enosh) takes over the theme in the later poetry.

The genealogy — Gen 5:1–5. "This is the book of the generations of adam." The chapter that matters to Aaron's Genesis 5 work: adam as species (v. 1–2) narrows to Adam the individual (v. 3–5), who begets Seth "in his own likeness, after his image" — the image language of 1:26 handed down a generation, in the text's own words. Enoch stands in this list (v. 18–24; his 365 years at v. 23) — inside the adam genealogy, which is the frame any calendrical reading of the number has to live in.

The blood of adam — Gen 9:5–6. "Whoever sheds the blood of adam, by adam shall his blood be shed, for in the image of God he made adam." Note the sound-field: dām (blood) inside the adam sentence. The text does not gloss it; the echo is there to be heard, and §6 treats it as wordplay, not proof.

adam in the Psalms — Ps 8:4–5 [Eng. 8:4–5]. "What is enosh that you remember him, and ben-adam (son of adam) that you attend to him?" The two human-words stand in parallel — the poet's own pairing of the species-word with the mortal-word. See the enosh study; this verse is the hinge between the two files.

adam as anyone — Eccl 7:20; Job; the wisdom books. "There is no righteous adam on earth who does good and never sins." In wisdom literature adam thins out to "anyone, a person, someone" — the species-word doing ordinary work, which is itself evidence of how basic the word is.

5. Wordplay — the adam sound-field

  • adam / adamah — Gen 2:7; 3:19. The master pun of the cluster.
  • adam / dam (blood) — Gen 9:5–6; also Lev 17:11's world, where the nephesh is in the dam. Later Jewish tradition makes the link explicit (adam read as aleph + dam in some readings; Genesis Rabbah plays on adam/adamah). Label: the echo is textual; the expansions are tradition.
  • adam / adom (red) / Edom — Gen 25:25, 30: Esau comes out אַדְמוֹנִי (ʾaḏmônî, "reddish") and is called Edom over the red stew. The narrator plainly enjoys the red-family sounds; whether אָדָם itself comes from "red" is the separate, unsettled question of §2.
  • adam / me'od — a rabbinic anagram: אָדָם rearranged is מְאֹד (məʾōḏ, "very" — as in "very good," Gen 1:31). Recorded in midrashic tradition (e.g. Genesis Rabbah). Beautiful, beloved, and a wordplay of the tradition, not of the biblical text itself — labelled accordingly.

6. Semitic cognates — where justified

  • Phoenician ʾdm, Sabaean ʾdm — "man, mankind" (BDB). The word is shared Northwest/South Semitic stock.
  • Arabic ʾādam — the name Adam, and ʾadamī, "human"; Arabic also has ʾadama, "skin/surface," compared by some lexica under adamah rather than here. The comparison is printed in BDB under adamah; do not move it across without saying so.
  • Assyrian admu — proposed, disputed (see §2). Not a settled cognate.
  • Caution: unlike nephesh and shem, adam has no clean, agreed Proto-Semitic pedigree. Anyone who gives you one is selling certainty the lexica do not have.

7. Ancient context

  • Clay-creation is the ancient world's common picture. In the Akkadian Atrahasis epic and in Enki-and-Ninmah traditions, humans are formed from clay — in Atrahasis, clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, so that something of the god lives in the human. Genesis 2:7's dust-plus-breath stands recognisably in that world and differs from it in its own way (one God, no slain god, breath not blood). Label: parallel, not source; scholars debate dependence, and this study takes no side it cannot document.
  • What the text claims vs. what tradition adds: the text claims the human is ground-made and God-breathed, male and female in the image. The traditions add: Adam's original size filling the world (b. Hagigah 12a — the same passage as the hidden-light tradition in the or study), Adam formed from dust gathered from the whole earth (Genesis Rabbah), and much more. All labelled tradition where used.

8. Translation alternatives

  • "man" (KJV and most) — accurate for the individual sense, but in current English it reads male, where the Hebrew word does not.
  • "humankind / mankind / human being" — better for Gen 1:26–27; 5:2, where the text itself spans both sexes.
  • "the human" — for הָאָדָם in Gen 2–3, preserving that the figure is the kind before he is a name.
  • "Adam" — only where the text has narrowed to the individual; translations differ on where that begins (some at Gen 2:20, some at 4:25), and the difference is a judgement call, not a textual fact.

9. Uncertainties, printed

  1. The etymology: red-root, ground-word, or old loan — unsettled (§2).
  2. Where generic adam becomes the proper name Adam inside Genesis 2–4 — debated by scholars, decided silently by every translation.
  3. The Assyrian admu comparison — proposed and disputed in BDB itself.
  4. Whether the adam/dam echo in Gen 9 is intended wordplay or coincidence of a small consonant inventory — the ear hears it; the text does not sign it.

Recall — three questions

Answer from memory first, then open each one to check. Drawn from this study’s own text.

Does אָדָם have a true plural?

No. The word is almost always singular in form; where English wants “men,” Hebrew uses בְּנֵי הָאָדָם, “children of the human” (Gen 11:5).

How often does adam occur, quoted honestly?

About 560 times (BDB’s heading). Other concordances land in the 550s; quote “about 560” and name the method.

Is the root of adam proven?

No — three candidates, none proven. Strong’s derives it from “to be red / ruddy”; the NAS concordance declines and calls the origin an unused word.
