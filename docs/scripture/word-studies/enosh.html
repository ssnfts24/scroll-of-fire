אֱנוֹשׁ — enosh — Full Hebrew Treatment (Finished, Nov 23, 2026)

Labels follow Aaron's rule: observation / interpretation / hypothesis / declaration are kept separate. This is the mortal-word of the adam cluster — read it with אָדָם (adam, the species) and אֲדָמָה (adamah, the ground). Where adam says what a human is made of and for, enosh says what a human is up against: he dies, he is frail, and he cannot cure himself.

1. The word itself

  • Consonantal text: אנוש (aleph–nun–vav–shin)
  • Pointed text: אֱנוֹשׁ
  • Transliteration: ʾĕnôš — "en-OSH"
  • Strong's / lexicon: H582; TWOT 136a. The proper name Enosh is a separate entry, H583.
  • Part of speech: masculine noun
  • Frequency — method matters here more than usual. The noun occurs 42 times in the Masoretic text by the usual concordance count, and it is heavily concentrated: BDB's own distribution note reads "mostly in poetry — 18 times in Job, 13 times in the Psalms," with most of the rest in Isaiah and a handful elsewhere. Some online concordances print numbers in the hundreds for this entry; those counts fold in other forms and the Aramaic cognate (Dan 7's bar enash and kin). Do not quote any count for enosh without its method; the poetry concentration, not the raw total, is the fact that matters for reading.
  • Forms: absolute אֱנוֹשׁ; construct in Jer 20:10 (אֱנוֹשׁ שְׁלוֹמִי, "the man of my peace" — my close friend). It is used both of individuals and collectively, and it has no plural of its own in ordinary use — אֲנָשִׁים (ʾănāšîm, "men") serves the wider root family as the plural of אִישׁ.

2. Root — the incurable root

Strong's and BDB both derive enosh from אָנַשׁ (ʾānaš, H605): "to be weak, sick — incurable." The verb survives in the text almost entirely in one fixed picture: a wound or sickness that cannot be healed.

  • Jer 15:18 — "my wound is incurable (אֲנוּשָׁה, ʾănûšâ)."
  • Jer 30:12 — "your hurt is incurable (אָנוּשׁ)."
  • Mic 1:9 — "her wounds are incurable (אֲנוּשָׁה)."
  • 2 Sam 12:15 uses the verb of David's child: "and he became sick (וַיֵּאָנַשׁ)."
  • Jer 17:9 — the root lands on the human heart itself: "the heart is deceitful above all things, and incurably sick (וְאָנֻשׁ הוּא) — who can know it?" The adjective there is the enosh root, predicated of the heart. See §5.

Strong's gloss of the noun follows the root honestly: "properly, a mortal — and thus differing from the more dignified adam." That contrast is Strong's editorial phrasing, but the distribution backs the substance: enosh is the word the poets reach for when the human is being measured against God and found frail.

  • Observation: the derivation from the "incurable" root is the lexica's standard account, stated without BDB's "perhaps."
  • Caution anyway: etymology is not meaning-in-use. Enosh in a given verse means "man/mankind"; the frailty is carried by the contexts (§4) first and the root second. Do not preach the root where the verse is doing something else.

3. Semantic range (BDB)

  1. An individual man — Job 5:17 ("happy is the enosh whom God reproves"); Ps 55:14 [Eng. 55:13]; Jer 20:10 (the friend).
  2. Men collectively, ordinary men — Isa 24:6; 33:8; Ps 73:5; and the telling idiom of Isa 8:1, חֶרֶט אֱנוֹשׁ — "a common stylus," an ordinary, human-sized pen: enosh as the measure of the commonplace.
  3. Man, mankind under the aspect of mortality — the dominant poetic sense: Deut 32:26; Job 7:1, 17; 14:19; 28:13; Isa 51:12.

Enosh never names the species in its dignity (that is adam), never marks maleness (that is ish), and is never a word of law or narrative prose if poetry will do. It is the poets' word for us.

4. Biblical examples — Genesis first, then the poetry where it lives

Enosh the man — Gen 4:26 (H583, the proper name). "To Seth also a son was born, and he called his name Enosh. Then it was begun to call on the name of YHWH." The third generation from Adam carries the mortal-word as a name — and the calling-on-the-Name begins in his days. The text states the two facts side by side and glosses neither. What tradition made of that pairing is §7, and it pulls in two opposite directions.

The hinge verse — Ps 8:4–5 [Eng. 8:4–5]. "What is enosh that you remember him, and ben-adam that you attend to him?" Both human-words in one line of parallel poetry: enosh for the frail one, ben-adam for the species-son. If one verse teaches the difference between the two words, it is this one — and the wonder of the psalm is that the frail one is remembered at all.

Job's enosh — Job 7:17; 15:14; 25:4–6. Job takes the psalm's question and turns it: "What is enosh, that you make so much of him?" (7:17). Eliphaz: "What is enosh, that he should be pure?" (15:14). Bildad lands the root's full weight: "how much less enosh, who is a worm" (25:6). Job is enosh's home book — 18 of its 42 occurrences — because Job is the book about a mortal who cannot cure his case.

The grass parallel — Ps 90:3; 103:15. "You return enosh to contrition" (90:3). "As for enosh, his days are like grass" (103:15). Enosh is the word chosen when the lifespan itself is the subject.

Do not fear enosh — Isa 51:7, 12. "Do not fear the reproach of enosh... who are you, that you fear enosh who dies, and ben-adam who is made like grass?" The prophet uses the frailest word for man precisely to shrink the threat: the thing you fear is an enosh.

The incurable heart — Jer 17:9. See §2 and §5: the root's adjective on the heart. Jeremiah's diagnosis is the enosh condition stated as anthropology.

Asa's prayer — 2 Chron 14:11 [Eng. 14:10]. "Let not enosh prevail against you" — a king begging that mortal strength not be the measure of the battle.

The Aramaic cousin on stage — Dan 7:13. כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, "one like a son of man" — literally "like a bar enash," the Aramaic cognate (H1247). Greek-first rule for the New Testament applies to its later echoes; inside Daniel the phrase is Aramaic and means, at face value, "one in human form" set against the beasts. What later tradition built on it is labelled tradition wherever this set of studies touches it.

5. Wordplay

  • enosh / anash (incurable) — the live wire of this file: Jer 17:9 predicates the root of the heart; Jer 15:18 and 30:12 of wounds; Mic 1:9 of a city's stripes. The poets did not need the etymology explained to them; the adjective and the noun share a page often enough (Jeremiah especially) that the echo is part of the book's music. Observation: the echo is textual. Interpretation: enosh is "man as the incurable patient." The second sentence is a reading, not a gloss — keep them separate.
  • enosh / anashim / ish — the wider family (אִישׁ, אֲנָשִׁים, אִשָּׁה) shares the aleph-nun-shin neighbourhood in the dictionaries' arrangement, but the historical relations among them are debated. Print the neighbourhood; do not draw the family tree as fact.
  • Enosh / "then it was begun" — Gen 4:26's אָז הוּחַל (ʾāz hûḥal, "then it was begun") is read by a famous rabbinic line as from חִלֵּל, "to profane" — "then the Name was profaned" (Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah). The plain reading takes it from חָלַל in its "begin" sense as the Hophal "it was begun." Same consonants, two sermons. §7 keeps them apart.

6. Semitic cognates — where justified

This word has the clean Semitic pedigree adam lacks:

  • Aramaic אֱנָשׁ (ʾĕnāš) — "man, mankind" (collective), the everyday word in Daniel's Aramaic and in Targum; Nabataean and Palmyrene inscriptions likewise (BDB).
  • Arabic ʾunās / nās — "people, mankind" (collective).
  • Akkadian nišu — "people," and tenišētu, "humanity, the human race" (BDB, citing the standard Assyrian references).
  • Sabaean ʾns — attested (BDB).
  • Observation: across the family, the word-family leans collective — peoples, humanity — which fits the Hebrew usage: enosh is rarely one hero; it is us, together, in our condition.

7. Traditions — the two readings of Enosh's generation

Gen 4:26 is one verse, and tradition split on it cleanly:

  • The plain line: in Enosh's days, public calling on YHWH's name began — the start of gathered worship. So the verse reads at face value, and so many commentators, ancient and modern, take it.
  • The rabbinic line: Genesis Rabbah and Rashi read הוּחַל as profanation — in Enosh's generation men began calling idols by the Name, and idolatry entered the world. Maimonides builds his whole history of idolatry on this (Mishneh Torah, Avodat Kokhavim 1:1): in the days of Enosh, mankind fell into the error that became worship of intermediaries.
  • This study's handling: both are printed because both are real traditions with named sources. Neither is the verse's own gloss — the verse gives none. Aaron's Genesis work should cite the reading it uses, by name, every time.

8. Translation alternatives

  • "mortal man / mortal" — the most honest rendering of the poetic sense (so many modern versions at Ps 8:4: "what is man" flattens it; "what is a mortal" keeps the edge).
  • "man / mankind" — safe and colourless; loses the frailty the contexts carry.
  • "human being" — acceptable; still loses the Job-and-Jeremiah incurability echo, which no single English word carries. A study Bible footnote is where this word's freight has to ride.

9. Uncertainties, printed

  1. The count: 42 by the usual concordance method; online figures vary wildly by what they fold in. Method named or number withheld (§1).
  2. The root derivation is standard but, as with all etymology, it explains the word's background, not each occurrence's sense.
  3. The ish/anashim family relations — neighbourhood certain, tree debated (§5).
  4. Gen 4:26 — worship's beginning or profanation's: the text does not decide; tradition divides (§7). Anyone who tells you the verse plainly says one of these is reading a tradition back into it — possibly rightly, but not plainly.
