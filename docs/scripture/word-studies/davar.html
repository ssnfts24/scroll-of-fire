דָּבָר — davar — Full Hebrew Treatment (Finished, Nov 23, 2026)

Labels follow Aaron's rule: observation / interpretation / hypothesis / declaration are kept separate. This is the eighth study on the shelf, and the word beneath Aaron's own name for the light of it — Or HaDabar, "the Light of the Word" (§10). The text's own data is marked as such; interpretation is labelled interpretation.

1. The word itself

  • Consonantal text: דבר (dalet–bet–resh)
  • Pointed text: דָּבָר
  • Transliteration: dāvār (commonly written dabar or davar)
  • Strong's / lexicon: H1697
  • Part of speech: masculine noun
  • Occurrences: roughly 1,440 times in the Hebrew Bible — among the most frequent nouns in the whole text.

2. Root and morphology

  • Root: ד־ב־ר (d-b-r). The verb dibbēr (דִּבֵּר, Piel) means "to speak"; the noun is what is spoken — and then, by the word's own reach, the thing spoken about.
  • Common forms:
    • דָּבָר — davar, a word / a matter
    • דְּבַר — devar-, "word of…" (construct: devar YHWH, "the word of YHWH")
    • דְּבָרִים — devarim, "words" — the Hebrew name of the book of Deuteronomy is simply דְּבָרִים, "Words."
    • דִּבְרֵי — divrei, "words of…" (divrei hayamim, "the matters of the days" — the Hebrew title of Chronicles is literally "Words/Matters of the Days": a chronicle is a book of devarim because words and events are the same noun.)

3. Semantic range — the heart of this word

Davar refuses to split what English splits. Its range:

  1. Word, speech, utterance — what is said.
  2. Command, promise, decree — what is said by one with authority.
  3. Matter, affair, business — the thing under discussion ("the matter of the king").
  4. Thing, event, occurrence — the thing itself, out in the world.

This is the word's documented centre, not a preacher's flourish: in Hebrew thought as the text itself uses it, a davar is a word with the weight of a thing. When YHWH speaks a davar, it does not describe reality from outside — it arrives as an event inside it (see §4, Isaiah 55:11).

4. Biblical examples

Genesis 15:1 — "The devar of YHWH came to Abram in a vision." The word comes, like a traveller or a messenger — it has movement and presence.

Deuteronomy 1:1 — "These are the devarim that Moses spoke." A whole book is a man's words, and the words are treated as events that carry a nation.

Exodus 34:28 — the tablets hold aseret hadevarim, "the Ten Words" — not "commandments" in the Hebrew. The covenant's core is ten words.

1 Samuel 3:1 — "Devar YHWH was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision." A famine measured not in bread but in word.

Psalm 33:6 — "Bidvar YHWH the heavens were made" — by the davar of YHWH. Creation by word, stated as mechanism, in poetry.

Isaiah 55:11 — "So shall my davar be that goes out from my mouth: it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish what I purpose." The word as something sent, travelling, working, and returning with a report.

5. Wordplay the text itself plays

  • דָּבָר / דֶּבֶר (davar / dever, "plague") — same letters, different vowels. Exodus 9:3 sends a dever on Egypt's livestock: the dark twin of the word — a "word" of judgment that is also a thing in the world.
  • מִדְבָּר (midbar, "wilderness") — built on the same root letters: the place where the word is heard. Israel receives the devarim in the midbar — the word-place. Traditional teachers have leaned on this for centuries: the wilderness is where speech becomes audible because everything else is quiet.
  • דְּבוֹרָה (Devorah, Deborah) — the prophetess's name is "bee," from the same root field: the creature whose hum is a kind of speech, and whose sting is a word that lands as a thing.

6. Semitic cognates (where justified)

  • Aramaic / Syriac: dabar carries "lead, guide, conduct" — the root's older undertone of arranging and directing what is spoken of.
  • Arabic: the root d-b-r gives tadbīr — management, arrangement, directing of affairs. Across the family, the root keeps joining speech to ordering: to speak a matter is to set it in order.
  • Ugaritic: dbr appears in the same speech/matter field. (Labelled: cognate evidence supports the speech↔order undertone; it does not prove any single verse's meaning.)

7. Translation alternatives

English Bibles must choose, verse by verse, among word / thing / matter / affair / command / promise — and the choice hides the unity. "Thing" in older translations (e.g., "what thing is this?") is not loose translation; it is the noun's own range. A reader of only English meets five words; a reader of the Hebrew meets one.

8. Traditions

  • Targum (Aramaic tradition): where the Hebrew has YHWH speaking or acting, the Targum often renders מֵימְרָא (Memra, "the Word") of YHWH — the Word as YHWH's own active presence going forth. This is the Hebrew-Aramaic soil in which later "Word" language grew; any New Testament comparison must start from the Greek text first (per Aaron's standing rule) and come to this background second, never the reverse.
  • Rabbinic tradition counts the world as created by ten utterances (Mishnah Avot 5:1) — ten devarim again, echoing the Ten Words of the tablets.

9. Uncertainties, honestly labelled

  • The exact pre-history of the root (whether "speak" or "arrange/order" is the older sense) is unresolved in the scholarship; both are ancient.
  • The davar/dever and midbar connections are real letter-and-root relationships; how much wordplay any given author intended in a given verse is interpretation, and is labelled so here.
  • The Memra tradition is documented; what it proves about any later text is a separate question, not settled by this study.

10. Aaron's lens (interpretation — his, recorded as his)

Aaron holds that words are programming — that speech sets order in the world, and modern marketing is a form of spellwork built on that fact. Davar is the textual ground that conviction stands nearest to: the one noun where the Hebrew itself will not let "word" and "thing" come apart. His own name for the light of it is already on the shelf: Or HaDabar — the Light of the Word. This study is filed as the word-study beneath that name.
