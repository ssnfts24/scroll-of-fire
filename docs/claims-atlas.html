Evidence and Research

Documentation Hub

Documented claims and documented facts, in one place.

The site's five maps, in order: the Hub (tools) · the Claims Atlas (evidence) · the Codex Atlas (everything) · the Lab Archive (the Lab's own index) · Search (the finder). This page is the second — where the evidence lives.

Government programs and court rulings. Corporate judgments, pleas, and fines. The Epstein record. Nikola Tesla. The orphan trains. The Old World as the record states it. And the Codex's own claim register, graded claim by claim.

The bar for this page. Nothing stands here without a named, checkable source that was opened and read. Every entry carries its evidence grade. An allegation in a court filing is labeled as exactly that — it is never upgraded to a fact. What was observed is kept apart from what it might mean, and where a common reading outruns the document, the entry says so.

Learn the method behind the grades. The Witness Method is a short lesson that teaches the five evidence labels by using them — you sort real claims from this hub's own Old World investigation, including the ones that failed their check, and the lesson shows the investigation's reasoning for each. About six minutes, kept on your device only.

The pattern in the record

Before the list, the shape of it. Every entry in this hub stands on the chart below as a symbol — the symbol says what kind of thing it is: a fire, a fair, a star fort, children moved, a court record. The shading around it says how well it is proven. The two never merge: a pattern built on allegations can never look like a pattern built on documents.

Travel sideways through the years. Tap any symbol to stand at that entry. Open a stack or a thread and it reads in order — what came first, what followed, what stood at the end — because a pattern is a sequence, not just a pile. And the labels stay honest: a documented correlation means the records overlap or share a name, and that is all it means. Where the record shows only overlap and no order, the reading says exactly that. Patterns that were checked and did not hold are kept at the bottom, labeled — the checking is part of the record.
